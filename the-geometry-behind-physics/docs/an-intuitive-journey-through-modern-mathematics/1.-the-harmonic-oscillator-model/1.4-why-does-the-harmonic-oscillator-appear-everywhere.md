# 1.4 Why does the harmonic oscillator appear everywhere?

At this point, we have found the harmonic oscillator in several apparently unrelated places:

* a mass attached to a spring,
* an RLC electrical circuit,
* an electromagnetic field mode.

This is not an accident. The deeper reason comes from what happens near a stable equilibrium.

Suppose a physical system has potential energy $$(V(x))$$, with a stable equilibrium at $$(x_0)$$.

At equilibrium,&#x20;

$$
\frac{dV}{dx}=0
$$

We can expand the potential near $$(x_0)$$:

$$
V(x)
\approx
V(x_0)
+
\frac{1}{2}
\frac{d^2V}{dx^2}
(x-x_0)^2
$$

The linear term disappears because the first derivative is zero at equilibrium. If we define

$$
k=
\frac{d^2V}{dx^2}
$$

then near equilibrium,

$$
V(x)
\approx
V(x_0)
+
\frac{1}{2}k(x-x_0)^2
$$

which is precisely the restoring force of a harmonic oscillator.

This explains why the harmonic oscillator appears so frequently in physics. Whenever a system experiences a small displacement around a stable equilibrium, the lowest-order approximation is often _**harmonic**_.

A molecule vibrating around its equilibrium geometry behaves approximately like a harmonic oscillator.

Atoms vibrating around their lattice positions in a crystal behave approximately like harmonic oscillators.

Small electromagnetic field modes behave like harmonic oscillators.

Many complicated physical systems therefore reduce, at least locally, to the same simple mathematical model. The harmonic oscillator is not important because springs are important. It is important because **quadratic behavior is the universal first approximation near a stable minimum**.
