# 1.6 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator: Symmetry, Nodes, and Hermite Polynomials

We have now seen how the harmonic-oscillator equation appears in classical mechanics, electrical circuits, and electromagnetic fields.

The next question is unavoidable:

> **What happens when the harmonic oscillator itself becomes a quantum system?**

There are several standard ways to solve the quantum harmonic oscillator. One can begin directly from the Schrödinger differential equation and derive the Hermite-polynomial solutions. That approach is rigorous and important, but when I first learned the subject, I found another way of thinking about the problem much more illuminating.

The perspective developed in this section is inspired by the way **Prof. Francis Spano** taught the quantum harmonic oscillator in my quantum chemistry studies. Rather than treating the Hermite polynomials as special functions that mysteriously appear at the end of a differential-equation calculation, we begin with the lowering and raising operators, solve the lowest-energy state, and then use **symmetry, parity, and the number of nodes** to anticipate what the higher-energy wavefunctions must look like in the next section.

This way of thinking changed how I understood the harmonic oscillator.

Instead of asking only

> “How do we solve this differential equation?”

we can ask

> **“What must see what the wavefunction look like before we solve it?”**

That question will lead us naturally from the ground-state Gaussian to the Hermite polynomials.

***

#### The Hamiltonian and the ladder operators

The one-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator is described by the Hamiltonian

$$
\hat{H}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^2x^2=\frac{\hat{P}^2}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^2\hat{X}^2 \qquad (1.6.1)
$$

where the momentum operator $$\hat P$$ is

$$
\hat P = -i\hbar \frac{d}{dt} \qquad (1.6.2)
$$

The stationary Schrödinger equation is

$$
\hat{H}\psi_n(x)=E_n\psi_n(x)  \qquad (1.6.3a)
$$

We can write it in Dirac notation as

$$
\hat{H} \ket{n} =E_n \ket{n}  \qquad (1.6.3b)
$$

We could solve this second-order differential equation directly. Instead, let us factor out $$\hbar\omega$$ , and rewrite the Hamiltonian in Eq. 1.6.1 as

$$
\hat{H}=  \hbar\omega [\frac{\hat{P}^2}{2m \hbar\omega} + \frac{m\omega}{2 \hbar}\hat{X}^2]  \qquad (1.6.4)
$$

Checking the dimensions of the constants, you can readily verify that:

$$
[  \hbar\omega]=\text{energy}, [2m \hbar\omega]=\text{momentum}^2,  [\frac{2 \hbar}{m\omega}]=\text{length}^2
$$

That means the quantity in the bracket of Eq. 1.6.4 is dimensionless (pure number with unit 1). We can then introduce the dimensionless quantities:

$$
\hat{\xi}=\sqrt{ \frac{m\omega}{2 \hbar}}\hat{X}  \qquad (1.6.5a)
$$

$$
\hat{\eta}=\frac{\hat{P}}{\sqrt{2m \hbar\omega}} \qquad (1.6.5b)
$$

Then the Hamiltonian becomes:

$$
\hat{H}=  \hbar\omega[\hat{\xi}^2+ \hat{\eta}^2]  \qquad (1.6.6)
$$

The operators $$\hat{\xi}$$ and $$\hat{\eta}$$  are simply the position and the momentum operators rescaled by\
some real constants; therefore both of them are Hermitean. Their commutation relation can\
be easily computed using the canonical commutation relations:

$$
[\hat{\xi}, \hat{\eta}]=\frac{1}{2\hbar}[\hat{X},\hat{P}]=\frac{i}{2} \qquad (1.6.7)
$$

If operators $$\hat{\xi}$$ and $$\hat{\eta}$$  are **commuting** variables, we would be tempted to factorize the Hamiltonian as:

$$
\hat{H}=  \hbar\omega(\hat{\xi}+i\hat{\eta})(\hat{\xi}-i\hat{\eta}) \qquad (1.6.8)
$$

We **must be careful** here, because the operators do not commute and generally speaking the equation 1.6.7 is **WRONG** here. So let us introduce operators of  $$\hat{a}$$ and $$\hat{a}^\dagger$$ and check if we can reproduce Eq. 1.6.6 with them:

$$
\hat{a}=\hat{\xi}+i\hat{\eta} \qquad (1.6.9a)
$$

$$
\hat{a}^\dagger=\hat{\xi}-i\hat{\eta} \qquad (1.6.9b)
$$

the expressions for $$\hat{a}$$ and $$\hat{a}^\dagger$$ in terms of  $$\hat{X}$$ and $$\hat{P}$$  are&#x20;

$$
\hat{a}=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\hat{X}+{\frac{i}{\sqrt{2m\omega\hbar}}}\hat{P}  \qquad (1.6.10a)
$$

$$
\hat{a}^\dagger=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\hat{X}-{\frac{i}{\sqrt{2m\omega\hbar}}}\hat{P} \qquad (1.6.10b)
$$

We can compute:

$$
\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger=\hat{\xi}^2+i[\hat{\eta},\hat{\xi} ]+ \hat{\eta}^2  \qquad (1.6.11a)
$$

$$
\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}=\hat{\xi}^2-i[\hat{\eta},\hat{\xi} ]+ \hat{\eta}^2  \qquad (1.6.11b)
$$

Thus, summing Eqs. 1.6.11a with 1.6.11b above, we have

$$
\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger+\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}=2[\hat{\xi}^2+ \hat{\eta}^2]  \qquad (1.6.12)
$$

We can substitute Eq. 1.6.12 into 1.6.6 as:

$$
\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2} \hbar\omega(\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger+\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}) \qquad (1.6.12)
$$

Subtracting the same two equations yields the commutation relation between operators of  $$\hat{a}$$ and $$\hat{a}^\dagger$$ (see Eq. 1.6.7):

$$
\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger-\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}\equiv [\hat{a}, \hat{a}^\dagger]=2i*(-\frac{i}{2} )=1  \qquad (1.6.13)
$$

This commutation relations plays an important role in the rest of this section. From it, we can eliminate the term, $$\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger$$ , in Eq. 1.6.12 as:

$$
\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2} \hbar\omega(\hat{a}\hat{a}^\dagger+\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a})=\frac{1}{2} \hbar\omega(1+\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a})=(\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+\frac{1}{2})\hbar\omega \qquad (1.6.12)
$$

#### Eigensystem: Eigenvalues and Eigenfunctions

It should be clear that repeated application of the lowering operator, $$\hat{a}$$, generates states of\
successively lower energy ad infinitum unless there is a state of lowest energy; application of\
the operator to such a state must yield zero identically (because otherwise we would be able\
to generate another state of lower energy still, a contradiction).

Is there such a state? The answer is yes because the Hamiltonian can only have positive\
eigenvalues. Consider the expectation value of $$\hat{H}$$ in an arbitrary state $$\ket{\Psi}$$:

$$
\langle \hat{H} \rangle= \langle \frac{\hat{p}^2}{2m }  \rangle +\langle \frac{m\omega^2}{2 }\hat{x}^2 \rangle  \qquad (1.6.13)
$$

and both terms on the right hand side are non-negative. Thus there cannot be any states of\
negative energy. We denote the state of lowest energy, or ground state, by $$\ket{0}$$. Then since there cannot be a state of lower energy,

$$
\hat{a}\ket{0}=0  \qquad (1.6.14)
$$

$$
\hat{H} \ket{0}=\hbar\omega(\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}+\frac{1}{2})\ket{0}=\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega\ket{0}\equiv E_0 \ket{0}  \qquad (1.6.15)
$$

Thus we have found the ground state energy:

$$
E_0=\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega  \qquad (1.6.16)
$$

Application of the raising operator to the ground state generates the state $$\ket{1}$$ with energy $$E_1=\frac{3}{2}\hbar\omega$$, whilst n applications of the raising operator generates the state $$\ket{n}$$ with energy $$(n+\frac{1}{2})\hbar\omega$$, so that

$$
E_n=(n+\frac{1}{2})\hbar\omega \ \ \ \ n=0,1,2,3,\cdots  \qquad (1.6.17)
$$

Now, let's focus on the analytic expression for the eigenfunctions of this model.  The ground state is defined by the relation of Eq. 1.6.14, we can rewrite it as a differential operator acting on the wave function of the ground state $$\psi_0(x)$$:

$$
\hat{a} \psi_0(x)=[\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\hat{X}+{\frac{i}{\sqrt{2m\omega\hbar}}}\hat{P}] \psi_0(x)=[\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}x+\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2m\omega}}\frac{d}{dx}]\psi_0(x)=0 \qquad (1.6.18)
$$

After reorganization, we have&#x20;

$$
\frac{d}{dx} \psi_0(x)=-\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}x \psi_0(x)  \qquad (1.6.18)
$$

We can set $$\alpha^2= \frac{m\omega}{\hbar}$$ for simplicity:

$$
\frac{d}{dx} \psi_0(x)=- \alpha^2 x \psi_0(x)  \qquad (1.6.19)
$$

The solution of the equation above is the Gaussian function:

$$
\psi_0(x) =C_0 exp[-\frac{ \alpha^2 x^2}{2}]  \qquad (1.6.20)
$$

The coefficient $$C_0$$ is the normalization factor. Interestingly, the ground state wavefunction has even parity symmetry without any node. And the ground-state probability density is concentrated near the center and decays as we move away from equilibrium.

Every other eigenfunctions is obtained by repeatedly applying the creation operator $$\hat{a}^\dagger$$ to the ground state wavefunction $$\ket{0}$$ and we will get the so-called Hermite polynomials. However, as I have mentioned, I don't want to show the rigorous derivation. Instead, it is more important for us to understand the geometric meaning of them and we can remember them easily.&#x20;
