# 1.7 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator: Symmetry, Nodes, and Hermite Polynomials (Continued)

In the previous section, we used the lowering operator to obtain the Gaussian ground-state wavefunction and introduced the idea that the raising operator generates the higher-energy states.

Now we want to understand something more visual:

> **Before doing the full algebra, what can symmetry and geometry already tell us about the excited-state wavefunctions?**

This question brings me back to something I heard repeatedly during graduate school in China. One professor who taught quantum chemistry often complained that the mathematics learned in high school was too elementary and that graduate students needed to “move forward” to more advanced mathematics. There is certainly some truth in the idea that advanced physics requires more advanced mathematical tools.

But I gradually realized that this does not mean the earlier mathematics becomes useless.

**Quite the opposite.**

Sometimes the simplest ideas from high-school mathematics remain the best way to understand what the more complicated formulas are actually doing. The harmonic oscillator gives a particularly nice example.

In this section, we will see that ideas from **analytic geometry** — symmetry about the origin, odd and even functions, the shape of a parabola, shifting a curve up or down, and counting where a function crosses the horizontal axis — already tell us a surprising amount about the structure of the Hermite polynomials.

This is one of the recurring ideas of this book:

> **Advanced mathematics does not replace simple mathematics. It often builds on it.**

The point is not to remain at the level of elementary geometry. The point is to use the simplest correct picture first, and then let the formal mathematics explain why that picture works.

In the following subsections, we will use this idea to understand the parity, nodes, and polynomial structure of the quantum harmonic-oscillator eigenstates, and from there see why the Hermite polynomials have the form they do.

#### Before climbing the oscillator ladder, remember the particle in a box

Now comes the part that gives us a more intuitive picture of the excited states. Consider the one-dimensional infinite square well. Introductory textbooks often place the well between

$$
0<x<L,  \qquad (1.7.1)
$$

with the corresponding eigenfunctions

$$
\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}
\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right),
\qquad
n=1,2,3,\ldots  \qquad (1.7.2)
$$

This is mathematically convenient, but the choice of origin hides the parity symmetry. Instead, move the center of the box to the origin:

$$
-\frac{L}{2}<x<\frac{L}{2}. \qquad (1.7.3)
$$

Now we can find the potential well is symmetric under the parity operation (inversion):

$$
V(-x)=V(x), x\rightarrow -x. \qquad (1.7.4)
$$

The wavefunction now becomes

$$
\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}
\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right),
\qquad
n=1,2,3,\ldots  \qquad (1.7.5)
$$

We can see the ground state ($$n=1$$) is symmetric under parity operation with 0 node. The first excited state is antisymmetric under parity operation with 1 node. We can plot it in the figure.&#x20;

To understand this parity problem, we can define the parity operator ($$\hat{P}$$) by

$$
\hat{P}\psi(x)=\psi(-x). \qquad (1.7.6)
$$

For an energy eigenstate of a one-dimensional symmetric potential, the wavefunction can also be chosen as an eigenstate of parity:

$$
\hat{P}\psi(x)=p\psi(x)  \qquad (1.7.7)
$$

where $$p$$ is the eigenvalue of the the parity operator $$\hat{P}$$. Therefore,

$$
\psi(-x)=p\psi(x).  \qquad (1.7.8)
$$

Now apply the parity operation twice will change $$x\rightarrow -x$$ and then change it back again must return the original wavefunction:

$$
\hat{P}^2\psi=\psi.  \qquad (1.7.9)
$$

Therefore,&#x20;

$$
p^2=1.  \qquad (1.7.10)
$$

So the possible parity eigenvalues are only $$p=\pm1$$. If $$p=+1,$$ then $$\psi(-x)=\psi(x),$$ and the wavefunction is **even**. If  $$p=-1,$$ then $$\psi(-x)=-\psi(x),$$ and the wavefunction is **odd**. So the symmetry of the potential tells us that the eigenfunctions naturally fall into two families: $$\text{even states}$$ and $$\text{odd states}.$$

#### Where does the alternating pattern come from?

Parity alone tells us that each eigenstate is either even or odd. But why do the states appear in the sequence $$\text{even} \rightarrow \text{odd} \rightarrow \text{even} \rightarrow \text{odd} \rightarrow \cdots$$ rather than, for example, two even states followed by two odd states?

For one-dimensional bound-state problems, the energy eigenstates are ordered by their number of internal nodes. The ground state has $$0$$ node. The first excited state has $$1$$ node. The third excited state has $$2$$ nodes, and so on.

Now combine this with parity. An odd wavefunction must satisfy

$$
\psi(0)=0,  \qquad (1.7.11a)
$$

because

$$
\psi(0)=-\psi(0).  \qquad (1.7.11b)
$$

Therefore every odd state automatically has a node at the origin. An even state does not have to vanish at the origin. This gives the natural sequence.

1. The ground state has no nodes, so it must be even.
2. The first excited state has one node. For a symmetric potential, that node lies at the center, so the state is odd.
3. The second excited state has two nodes, placed symmetrically at $$x=\pm x_0,$$ so the state is even again.
4. The third excited state has three nodes: one at the origin and two symmetric nodes away from the center. It is therefore odd.

This analysis (Eqs. 1.7.11a and 1.7.11b) comes from the 1st year high school mathematical text book, right?&#x20;

So the pattern becomes

| State                | Number of nodes | Parity |
| -------------------- | --------------- | ------ |
| Ground state         | 0               | even   |
| First excited state  | 1               | odd    |
| Second excited state | 2               | even   |
| Third excited state  | 3               | odd    |

The alternation is therefore not accidental. It follows from combining two facts: $$\text{parity symmetry}$$ and $$\text{node ordering}.$$ Together they give $$\text{even} \rightarrow \text{odd} \rightarrow \text{even} \rightarrow \text{odd} \rightarrow \cdots$$

#### What about the probability density?

There is another interesting consequence. For an even state, $$\psi(-x)=\psi(x),$$ while for an odd state, $$\psi(-x)=-\psi(x).$$ But after taking the absolute square,$$|\psi(-x)|^2=|\psi(x)|^2.$$ So the probability density is symmetric in both cases. This means that even and odd wavefunctions can look very different in sign, but they produce probability densities with the same left-right symmetry: $$|\psi(x)|^2.$$ This is an important reminder that the sign or phase of a wavefunction contains information that disappears when we look only at the probability density.

#### The harmonic oscillator has the same parity symmetry

Now return to the harmonic potential: $$V(x)=\frac{1}{2}m\omega^2x^2.$$ Because $$V(-x)=V(x),$$ the harmonic oscillator has exactly the same parity symmetry as the centered particle-in-a-box potential. This does **not** mean that the two systems have the same wavefunctions or the same energy spectrum.&#x20;

**They do not.**

But it tells us something very important about the symmetry of their eigenstates. For the harmonic oscillator:

* the ground state is even and has zero nodes;
* the first excited state is odd and has one node;
* the second excited state is even and has two nodes;
* the third excited state is odd and has three nodes.

So even before calculating the excited-state wavefunctions, we already know what qualitative properties they must possess.

#### What should the first excited state of the harmonic oscillator look like?

We already found the eigenfunction of groud state:  $$\psi_0(x) =C_0 exp[-\frac{ \alpha^2 x^2}{2}]$$ in Eq. 1.6.20. As mentioned earlier, the Gaussian is even: $$\psi_0(-x)=\psi_0(x),$$ and it has no nodes. Now consider the first excited state. It must be:

* antisymmetric under parity;
* zero at (x=0);
* characterized by exactly one node.

Suppose the excited-state wavefunctions retain the same Gaussian envelope but acquire a polynomial factor:&#x20;

$$
\psi_n(x) =C_n P_n(x)  exp[-\frac{ \alpha^2 x^2}{2}]  \qquad (1.7.12)
$$

Because the Gaussian itself is even, the parity of the entire wavefunction is determined by $$P_n(x)$$.  For the first excited state, the simplest odd polynomial is

$$
P_1(x)\propto ax.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\psi_1(x) \propto C_1*( x)  exp[-\frac{ \alpha^2 x^2}{2}]  \qquad (1.7.13)
$$

The factor $$x$$ does exactly what we need. Under parity, $$x\rightarrow -x,$$ so the wavefunction changes sign. And because the value of the factor $$x$$ at $$x=0$$ does make the polynomial vanish, the state has one node at the center.

#### What should the second excited state look like?

The second excited state must be even again. If we check the first two terms of Hermite polynomials, they are $$1$$ for the ground state and $$ax$$ , respectively. We can suppose that the next term should be: $$P_2(x)\propto bx^2$$. This polynomial has the correct parity because $$b(-x)^2=bx^2.$$ But it does not have the correct node structure. The function $$bx^2$$  only touches zero at $$x=0$$. It does not change sign there, and it does not give us the two distinct nodes required for the second excited state.

So we need to shift the quadratic polynomial **downward**:

$$
P_2(x)=ax^2-b  \qquad (1.7.14)
$$

with $$a>0$$ and $$b>0$$ (again, the 1st year high school mathematical knowledge, right?).&#x20;

Now the zeros occur at $$x=\pm\sqrt{\frac{b}{a}}.$$ The polynomial remains even, but it now has two nodes. This is exactly the qualitative structure required for the second excited state. So before doing the detailed algebra, symmetry already suggests

$$
\psi_2(x) \propto C_2 *(ax^2-b)  exp[-\frac{ \alpha^2 x^2}{2}]  \qquad (1.7.15)
$$

And this is where the Hermite polynomials begin to emerge.

#### The Hermite-polynomial pattern

Define the dimensionless coordinate $$\xi=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}}x=\alpha x,$$ the harmonic-oscillator eigenfunctions can be written in the form $$\exp\left(-\frac{\xi^2}{2}\right).$$ The harmonic-oscillator eigenfunctions can be written in the form:

$$
\psi_n(x) =N_nH_n(\xi)
\exp\left(-\frac{\xi^2}{2}\right)   \qquad (1.7.16)
$$

where $$H_n(\xi)$$ is the $$n^{th}$$ Hermite polynomial. The first few terms are listed below:

So the Hermite polynomials are not arbitrary decorations added to the Gaussian function. Their parity and zeros encode exactly the symmetry and node structure required by the harmonic oscillator.

#### The raising operator generates the Hermite structure

The symmetry argument tells us what the wavefunctions should look like. The raising operator explains how they are generated. In terms of the dimensionless coordinate $$\xi$$,  $$\hat{a}^\dagger = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( \xi-\frac{d}{d\xi} \right).$$ Start from $$\psi_0(\xi) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{\xi^2}{2}\right),$$ we can apply $$\hat{a}^\dagger$$ on the ground state function once and it gives:

$$
a^\dagger\psi_0
\propto
\xi
\exp\left(-\frac{\xi^2}{2}\right)  \qquad (1.7.17)
$$

This is the first excited state. Applying $$\hat{a}^\dagger$$ again on it gives a quadratic polynomial multiplying the same Gaussian:

$$
(a^\dagger)^2\psi_0
\propto
(2\xi^2-1)
\exp\left(-\frac{\xi^2}{2}\right)  \qquad (1.7.18)
$$

Applying the raising operator repeatedly generates higher and higher polynomial factors. Those polynomial factors are proportional to the Hermite polynomials. So we can view the structure as $$\text{Gaussian ground state} \rightarrow \text{raising operator} \rightarrow \text{polynomial times Gaussian}.$$ The symmetry argument predicts what these polynomials must look like. The ladder operator generates them.
