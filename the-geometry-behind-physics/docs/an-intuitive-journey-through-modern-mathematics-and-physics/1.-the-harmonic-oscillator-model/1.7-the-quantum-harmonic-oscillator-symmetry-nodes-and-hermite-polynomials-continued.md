# 1.7 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator: Symmetry, Nodes, and Hermite Polynomials (Continued)

In the previous section, we used the lowering operator to obtain the Gaussian ground-state wavefunction and introduced the idea that the raising operator generates the higher-energy states.

Now we want to understand something more visual:

> **Before doing the full algebra, what can symmetry and geometry already tell us about the excited-state wavefunctions?**

This question brings me back to something I heard repeatedly during graduate school in China. One professor who taught quantum chemistry often complained that the mathematics learned in high school was too elementary and that graduate students needed to “move forward” to more advanced mathematics. There is certainly some truth in the idea that advanced physics requires more advanced mathematical tools.

But I gradually realized that this does not mean the earlier mathematics becomes useless.

**Quite the opposite.**

Sometimes the simplest ideas from high-school mathematics remain the best way to understand what the more complicated formulas are actually doing. The harmonic oscillator gives a particularly nice example.

In this section, we will see that ideas from **analytic geometry** — symmetry about the origin, odd and even functions, the shape of a parabola, shifting a curve up or down, and counting where a function crosses the horizontal axis — already tell us a surprising amount about the structure of the Hermite polynomials.
