# 1.3 From circuits to electromagnetic waves

So far, we have encountered the harmonic oscillator in two apparently very different systems, the mechanical oscillator and an RLC circuit.&#x20;

The physical meanings of the variables are completely different, but the mathematical structures are almost identical. Now we will encounter the same harmonic-oscillator structure in a less obvious place: **the electromagnetic field itself.**

This connection is particularly important because it eventually provides a bridge from classical electrodynamics to the quantum description of light.

Before talking about photons, we should first understand what happens to the electromagnetic field classically. In empty space, there are no free charges and no free currents. Therefore,

$$
\rho=0  \qquad{(1.3.1a)}
$$

and

$$
J=0 \qquad{(1.3.1b)}
$$

The source-free Maxwell equations can then be written, following the convention used in our reference notes, as

$$
\nabla\cdot \vec{E}=0 \ \ \ \text{(Gauss's law)} \qquad{(1.3.2a)}
$$

$$
\nabla\cdot \vec{B}=0 \ \ \ \text{(Gauss's law for magnetism)} \qquad{(1.3.2b)}
$$

$$
\nabla \times \vec{E}=-\frac{1}{c}
\frac{\partial \vec{B}}{\partial t} \ \ \ \text{(Maxwell-Faraday equation)} \qquad{(1.3.2c)}
$$

$$
\nabla \times B=\frac{1}{c}
\frac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t} \ \ \ \text{(Ampere’s circuital law)} \qquad{(1.3.2d)}
$$

Eqs. 1.3.2a and 1.3.2b tell us that, in empty space, neither the electric field nor the magnetic field has a local source. Eqs. 1.3.2c and 1.3.2d show something more interesting. A changing magnetic field generates a curling electric field,

$$
\vec{B}
\rightarrow
 \vec{E},
$$

while a changing electric field generates a curling magnetic field,

$$
\vec{E}
\rightarrow
 \vec{B}.
$$

The electric and magnetic fields are therefore coupled to each other. These are the same source-free Maxwell equations used in the reference notes before the wave equation is derived. Our next goal is to combine them into an equation involving only the electric field.

#### **Take the curl one more time**

Start with Faraday's law,

$$
\nabla \times \vec{E}=-\frac{1}{c}
\frac{\partial  \vec{B}}{\partial t}  \qquad{(1.3.2c)}
$$

Take the curl of both sides:

$$
\nabla \times\nabla \times \vec{E}=-\frac{1}{c}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
\left(
\nabla\times  \vec{B}
\right). \qquad{(1.3.3)}
$$

At this point we use an important vector identity:

$$
\nabla \times\nabla \times \vec{v}=\nabla (\nabla \cdot  \vec{v})-\nabla ^{2}\vec{v} \qquad{(1.3.4)}
$$

The left hand side of Eq. 1.3.3 becomes

$$
\nabla (\nabla \cdot  \vec{E})-\nabla ^{2}\vec{E}  \qquad{(1.3.5)}
$$

Substituting Gauss's law in Eq. 1.3.2a into Eq 1.3.5, Eq. 1.3.3 becomes&#x20;

$$
-\nabla ^{2}\vec{E}=-\frac{1}{c}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
\left(
\nabla\times  \vec{B}
\right) = -\frac{1}{c^2}
\frac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t}  \qquad{(1.3.6a)}
$$

The right hand side of Eq. 1.3.6 corresponds to the fact of Ampere’s circuital law. We can rewrite it:&#x20;

$$
\nabla ^{2}\vec{E}- \frac{1}{c^2}
\frac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t}=0  \qquad{(1.3.6b)}
$$

To solve this equation, the trick is to separate the spatial part from the time evolution part of the electromagnetic field

$$
E(\vec{r},t)=\sum_m
f_m(t)u_m(\vec{r})  \qquad{(1.3.7)}
$$

There are two separate ingredients here. The function $$u_m(\vec{r})$$ describes the spatial pattern of mode (m). The coefficient $$f_m(t)$$ describes how the amplitude of that mode changes with time. Substituting Eq. 1.3.7 into Eq. 1.3.6b, we have

$$
\sum_m
f_m(t)\nabla ^{2}u_m(\vec{r})- \frac{1}{c^2} \sum_m
[(\frac{\partial f_m(t)}{\partial t})u_m(\vec{r}) ]=0  \qquad{(1.3.8)}
$$

We can also choose the modes to satisfy the orthogonality condition (hence normal modes):

$$
\int u_m(\vec{r})u_n(\vec{r})d\vec{r}=\delta_{n,m}  \qquad{(1.3.9)}
$$

then we must have&#x20;

$$
f_m(t)\nabla ^{2}u_m(\vec{r})- \frac{1}{c^2}
(\frac{\partial f_m(t)}{\partial t})u_m(\vec{r}) =0  \qquad{(1.3.10)}
$$

We can collect the coordinate part on the left hand side and the time dependent part on the right hand side of the equation:&#x20;

$$
\frac{\nabla ^{2}u_m(\vec{r})} {u_m(\vec{r})}= \frac{1}{c^2} 
\frac{(\frac{\partial f_m(t)}{\partial t})}  { f_m(t)  }  \qquad{(1.3.10)}
$$

Since the two sides are independent to each other, they must equal to some constant, let's say $$-k^2 _m$$, we then have:

$$
{\nabla ^{2}u_m(\vec{r})} = -k^2 _m {u_m(\vec{r})}  \qquad{(1.3.11a)}
$$

$$
{(\frac{\partial f_m(t)}{\partial t})}=-{c^2} k^2 _m  { f_m(t)  }  \qquad{(1.3.11b)}
$$

These two equations also follow the harmonic oscillator equation with zero friction. In classical electrodynamics we watch photons as a bunch of harmonic oscillators in the vacuum.&#x20;
